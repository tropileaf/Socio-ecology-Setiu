This repository contains (anonymous) data and R code to understand how space and culture shape the conceptualisation of animal-related
health risks in Setiu Wetland mangroves.

Data analysis (long version) :

Quantitative data were entered into a spreadsheet and analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics in R software (R Core Team, version 4.6.0). Categorical variables such as animal sightings, types of contact and perceived risks were summarized descriptively, following approaches commonly used in community-based studies assessing human-animal interactions and zoonotic disease awareness, such as Nguyen et al. (2025) and Pérez-Pérez et al. (2025).  
Key variables such as occupation, mangrove use locations, wildlife encounter locations and perceived risks were grouped into broader analytical categories reflecting social-ecological contexts of human-wildlife interaction (Supplementary Material G). For statistical analyses, age was grouped into decadal categories (10–19, 20–29, 30–39, etc.) to facilitate comparisons across age groups while maintaining consistent interval widths between categories. These analytical age groups were independent of the legal distinction between adolescents (10–17 years) and adults (≥18 years) used for participant recruitment and ethical procedures. To account for differences in the number of species within each animal category, sighting frequencies were standardized by calculating the mean number of sightings per species. 

The 53 recorded species were classified into four categories representing a gradient of human association: domestic animals, synanthropic species, facultatively synanthropic species, and sylvatic species. Domestic animals included species under direct human management, whereas synanthropic species referred to non-domesticated animals strongly associated with human-modified environments. Facultatively synanthropic species were wildlife species capable of exploiting both natural and anthropogenic habitats, while sylvatic species were primarily associated with natural habitats and lower levels of human contact. 

Human exposure was evaluated using two complementary measures: exposure frequency and contact duration. Exposure frequency was quantified as the number of animal mentions per participant within each human-association category. To avoid pseudo replication resulting from multiple mentions by the same respondent, analyses were conducted at the participant level. Reported contact duration was standardized into ordinal exposure categories based on predefined time ranges reported as contact per week: no contact (0 minutes), very low (<5 minutes or passing encounters), low (5-30 minutes), high (>30 minutes), and constant exposure (all day/all night). Originally separate medium (30–120 minutes) and high (>120 minutes) categories were combined into a single high category for statistical analyses to improve model stability due to low frequencies in the upper exposure classes. This categorization allowed comparison of relative exposure intensity while minimizing variability from imprecise self-reporting. 

To test the first study hypothesis that human exposure to animals varies according to degree of human association, two complementary analyses were conducted. Differences in exposure frequency were assessed by comparing the standardized number of mentions per species per participant across human-association categories using a Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn post hoc pairwise comparisons with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. To account for repeated measures among participants, differences in contact duration (ordinal exposure category) were analyzed using a cumulative link mixed model (CLMM), with human-association category included as a fixed effect and participant identity as a random effect. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. 

To test the second study hypothesis that animal exposure varies across socio-demographic and occupational groups, CLMMs were employed because exposure time was measured as an ordinal response variable and included repeated observations from the same participants across multiple animal categories. These models assessed whether participant characteristics (occupation, age range and gender) influenced reported exposure time for each animal category. Participant identification number was included as a random effect to account for repeated observations within respondents. Occupation and age range reference categories were set to “Visitor” and “20-29”, respectively. The 20-29 age group was used as reference because it was the most represented age class in the dataset, improving model stability and interpretability. 

To assess whether demographic variables and animal categories influenced the likelihood of direct animal contact and by-product exposure, Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) with binomial error distributions were employed. They were considered appropriate because contact and by-product exposure variables were coded as binary outcomes and included repeated observations from the same participants. Animal category was included as a fixed effect, while participant identification number was included as a random effect. For contact models, “Fishing and related activities” was used as the occupation reference category, as this group was hypothesized to have higher levels of wildlife contact and associated health risks. 

To test the third hypothesis that perceptions of fauna and associated risks vary across demographic groups, separate binomial GLMMs were used to evaluate whether demographic factors influenced wildlife risk perception categories, including perceptions of animals as dangerous, pests, not dangerous, or friendly. Neutral perceptions were excluded from inferential analyses due to extremely low frequencies and complete separation across several demographic categories, which prevented stable model estimation. Perception categories were modeled independently due to the structure of the response variables. Occupation, age range, and gender were included as fixed effects, while participant identification number was included as a random effect to account for repeated observations from the same participants. Consistent with previous analyses, reference categories were set to “Visitor” for occupation and “20-29” for age range, because it provided the most stable baseline for comparison. Additional associations between demographic variables and perception variables were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. 

To test the fourth hypothesis that zoonotic disease awareness varies across demographic and occupational groups, bias-reduced regression approaches were used to assess whether demographic characteristics influenced zoonotic disease awareness. Firth-penalized logistic regression was applied to evaluate awareness of the Nipah virus as a binary outcome, while multinomial regression models with bias reduction were used to analyze broader zoonotic disease knowledge categories (no knowledge, awareness of animal diseases, and awareness of zoonotic diseases). These approaches were selected to reduce bias associated with sparse categorical responses, uneven category distributions, and low or zero counts in some response categories that resulted in quasi-separation. Occupation, age range, and gender were included as predictor variables. To improve parameter stability and interpretability under these conditions, unlike previous models, the 40-49 age group was used as the reference category for disease knowledge models, which improved parameter stability. 

For logistic, multinomial, and cumulative link models, effect sizes were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Odds ratios greater than 1 indicate increased odds of the outcome relative to the reference category, whereas odds ratios lower than 1 indicate decreased odds. An OR of 1 indicates no difference relative to the reference category. For categorical predictors, ORs were interpreted relative to the specified reference group for each model. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.
